Breeze

One web app, three jobs: customers order from shops in their zone, shop owners run their stock, and riders get matched to deliveries by fastest estimated arrival.

01

Full description

Breeze is a quick-commerce web app modelled on shops in Abuja (Wuse, Garki, Maitama and Kubwa). A customer picks a nearby shop, fills a cart, chooses a delivery address on a map and a payment option, and then follows the order through five stages, from "Order placed" to "Delivered".

The same app serves two other users. A shop owner sees revenue, orders and low-stock alerts, and can add, edit or remove products. A rider goes online, accepts or declines incoming orders and marks them delivered.

Breeze runs entirely in the browser. There is no server yet: data is stored in the browser's localStorage, payments are not actually processed, and order tracking is simulated with timers. It is built to demonstrate the full user flow and the dispatch logic, ready to be connected to a real backend.

02

The problem

Neighbourhood shops often run delivery through phone calls and WhatsApp messages. The customer doesn't know whether an item is in stock, how much delivery costs, or when the rider will arrive. The shop owner has no simple view of stock or orders, and riders get assigned by whoever answers first rather than whoever is closest.

Breeze puts all three sides in one place. Customers see stock and fees upfront, shop owners see what's selling and running low, and every order goes to the rider who can deliver it fastest.

How the rider gets picked

ETA = shop prep time + (rider → shop) + (shop → customer)

Riders in the shop's zone are shortlisted first (falling back to all riders if none are in the zone). Travel times assume an average speed of 25 km/h, and the rider with the lowest total ETA is assigned.

Features

  • Three roles, one login screenSign in as customer, shop owner or rider, with one-tap demo accounts and form validation (including Nigerian phone numbers).
  • Search and filter shopsSearch finds shops by name or by any product they stock; category chips narrow the list; closed shops show opening hours instead of a menu.
  • Stock-aware cart"Only 3 left" warnings, out-of-stock items disabled, quantities capped at available stock, and a prompt if you try to mix shops in one cart.
  • Mobile-first cartA sidebar cart on desktop becomes a floating button and slide-up drawer on phones.
  • 3-step checkout with a mapDelivery, payment and review steps; address search with Google Places (restricted to Nigeria), a draggable pin and reverse geocoding.
  • Automatic rider dispatchThe fastest-ETA rider in the shop's zone is assigned when the order is placed.
  • Order tracking and reorderA five-stage tracker with a moving rider marker, plus one-tap reorder that re-checks what's still in stock.
  • Shop owner dashboardRevenue, low-stock alerts, product add/edit/delete, an orders table with filters, and shop settings.
  • Rider viewOnline/offline toggle, accept or decline orders, and delivery and earnings counts.
  • Remembers your sessionCart, orders and products persist across page reloads; dark mode follows the device setting.

06

What I learned

  • Hooks have rules. My checkout page crashed until I moved every hook above the "cart is empty" early return. React needs hooks called in the same order on every render.
  • Snapshot state before you change it. When placing an order, I copy the cart first, then reduce stock and clear the cart. Reading the cart after clearing it gave the wrong totals.
  • Where you define a component matters. Defining the cart and item-form components inside their parents made inputs lose focus on every keystroke. Moving them to the top level fixed it.
  • Never trust saved data. localStorage can hold old or broken values, so the loader checks the shape of what it reads and falls back to defaults.
  • Clean up timers. The order tracker uses timeouts; clearing them when the page unmounts stopped updates firing on screens that no longer existed.
